## Findings

1. **Catastrophic Filter Collapse via "Black Swan" Observations**
   In partial-observation filtering, the active state space $\Omega(t)$ is determined prior to receiving the observation $y_t$ at time $t$. If an unexpected event occurs (e.g., a sudden super-spreading event or a reporting burst where $y_t$ exceeds the maximum infectious count $I$ present in $\Omega(t)$), the likelihood $p(y_t \mid x)$ will be zero (or underflow to numerical zero) for all $x \in \Omega(t)$. This causes a division-by-zero failure during the Bayesian update step:
   $$p(x \mid y_t) = \frac{p(y_t \mid x) p(x)}{\sum_{z \in \Omega(t)} p(y_t \mid z) p(z)}$$
   Unlike particle filters, which can recover via proposal distributions that incorporate the current observation, FSP's dynamic expansion is purely *forward-predictive* (propagating along the generator $Q$), leaving it blind to the observation during the expansion phase.

2. **Systematic Bias and Distortion of the Marginal Likelihood**
   The Finite State Projection (FSP) algorithm truncates the generator matrix $Q$ to a sub-matrix $Q_{\Omega \Omega}$, rendering the transition probability matrix $P_{\Omega}(t) = \exp(Q_{\Omega \Omega} t)$ sub-stochastic (row sums are $< 1$). The "lost" probability mass represents paths escaping $\Omega$. 
   If we renormalize the state probabilities at each observation step to sum to $1$, we artificially inflate the probability of the surviving states. This accumulates a severe, non-linear bias in the marginal likelihood estimator:
   $$\hat{p}(y_{1:T}) = \prod_{k=1}^T \left( \sum_{x \in \Omega(t_k)} p(y_k \mid x) \hat{p}(x \mid y_{1:k-1}) \right)$$
   This bias invalidates any downstream parameter estimation (such as Particle Marginal Metropolis-Hastings, PMMH) because the likelihood is systematically underestimated or wildly distorted depending on the varying truncation boundary.

3. **Topological Disconnection and Mode Amputation in Multimodal Posteriors**
   Low-population epidemic models often exhibit multimodality (e.g., one mode where the disease goes extinct rapidly, and another where it triggers a minor outbreak). Pruning states with posterior probability $p_x(t) < \delta$ will amputate the "valleys" of low probability between these modes. Because the dynamic expansion of $\Omega(t)$ only traces forward transition paths *starting from the current active set*, once a mode is pruned and topologically disconnected from the active set, the filter can never repopulate that region—even if subsequent observations strongly indicate the epidemic has resurged.

4. **The Computational Paradox of Dynamic Re-gridding for $N \le 100$**
   The motivation for FSP is reducing the $O(N^3)$ state-space size. However, for a low population (e.g., $N = 50$), the full SEIR state-space size is only $23,426$ states. 
   Maintaining a dynamic active set $\Omega(t)$ requires:
   * Graph traversal or breath-first search to identify the boundary states at each step.
   * Re-building the sparse matrix indices of $Q_{\Omega\Omega}$ in memory.
   * Solving a time-dependent ODE on a *changing* state space, which prevents the use of highly optimized, stationary matrix exponential solvers (like Expokit's Krylov subspace projection on a static matrix).
   The computational overhead of this dynamic management often exceeds the cost of a single, highly optimized static sparse matrix-vector multiplication on the full $O(N^3)$ space.

## Obstacles

* **Non-locality of the Observation Operator**: The observation model $Y_t \sim \text{Binomial}(I_t, \rho)$ or $\text{Poisson}(\lambda I_t)$ means that a single observation $y_t$ can instantly rule out large swaths of the state space (where $I_t < y_t$), while demanding the immediate inclusion of states with $I_t \ge y_t/\rho$ that may have been pruned due to low prior probability.
* **Loss of Conservativeness**: Standard numerical analysis bounds the FSP error in the $l_1$-norm for a *forward* master equation. In the presence of intermittent, noisy updates (filtering), there are no existing mathematical guarantees that the posterior error remains bounded by $\epsilon$ over long time horizons $T$.
* **Stiff Coupling at the Boundary**: Near the extinction boundary $I \to 0$, the transition rates change drastically (the infection rate drops to zero, while recovery rates remain active). This stiffness makes the boundary of $\Omega(t)$ highly sensitive to small changes in $\delta$, leading to artificial numerical oscillations in the active state space size.

   To prevent catastrophic filter collapse when observations fall outside the active grid $\Omega(t)$, modify the observation likelihood to be a mixture model:
   $$p(y_t \mid x) = (1 - \omega) p_{\text{nominal}}(y_t \mid x) + \omega p_{\text{outlier}}(y_t)$$
   where $\omega \ll 1$ is a small probability of an unmodeled anomaly. This guarantees a non-zero denominator and allows the filter to survive transient mismatches between the truncated grid and the data.

2. **Develop an "Observation-Guided" Dynamic Expansion**
   Instead of expanding $\Omega(t)$ purely based on the forward prior generator $Q_{\Omega\Omega}$, implement an expansion step that takes the current observation $y_t$ into account. Before computing the matrix exponential, expand the state space to include any state $x$ that is within $k$ transitions of $\Omega(t)$ *and* satisfies $p(y_t \mid x) > \tau$. This ensures the grid is proactively stretched toward the incoming data.

Krylov methods project the action of the full matrix exponential $\exp(Q t) v$ onto a small subspace of size $m \ll N^3$ without discarding states or losing probability conservation, avoiding all the overhead of dynamic grid tracking.
